# Wagon West: sources

Every fact read aloud in the game (the thirteen lookouts and the eight nations' trading-post facts) is listed here
with where it comes from, so a parent can open the source and check it. The sources are the National Park Service's
Oregon National Historic Trail and park pages, the Oregon-California Trails Association, the nations' own websites,
and state historical societies.

How to use this page: each entry names the organisation and the page or topic to look for. A web address is given
only where we are confident it exists; see the "Fact check" section at the end for the addresses checked on October 31, 2026. The
game states no number it could not trace: where a source gave a range or was unclear, the number was left out
(for example, the miles between two stops, or Independence Rock's height, are not read aloud).

Wording rules the content follows (SPEC section 1): nobody dies or is ill in the story; hunting is a cartoon bow;
Native nations are trading partners and guides, named as living peoples, in the present tense where that is true
today; each nation fact is two sentences at most and uses the name the nation uses for itself.

## Lookouts

- <a id="independence"></a>**independence**: National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail (nps.gov/oreg),
  the pages on the trail's beginning at the Missouri River towns and on when parties left in spring. Also the
  Oregon-California Trails Association (octa-trails.org), the "Trail" and "History" pages: the trail ran about 2,000
  miles, and parties left when the prairie grass was tall enough for the animals.
- <a id="kansas-river"></a>**kansas-river**: Kansas Historical Society (kshs.org), Kansapedia, the entries on the
  Oregon Trail and on ferries and crossings in Kansas: local families ran ferries that carried wagons across for a fee.
- <a id="fort-kearny"></a>**fort-kearny**: Nebraska State Historical Society (history.nebraska.gov), the pages on
  Fort Kearny State Historical Park: founded in 1848, with a blacksmith shop and a post office. The National Park
  Service Oregon National Historic Trail pages on Fort Kearny give the same dates.
- <a id="chimney-rock"></a>**chimney-rock**: National Park Service, Chimney Rock National Historic Site
  (nps.gov/chro): a spire that rises hundreds of feet above the valley (sources give 286 to 480 feet, so no number is read aloud), and the many diary entries by travelers.
- <a id="fort-laramie"></a>**fort-laramie**: National Park Service, Fort Laramie National Historic Site
  (nps.gov/fola), the history pages: founded in 1834 as a fur trading post, bought by the army in 1849.
- <a id="independence-rock"></a>**independence-rock**: National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail
  (nps.gov/oreg), the page on Independence Rock; Wyoming State Parks and Historic Sites, "Independence Rock State
  Historic Site"; Oregon-California Trails Association: travelers carved or painted their names on the granite.
- <a id="south-pass"></a>**south-pass**: National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail (nps.gov/oreg), the
  page on South Pass; Bureau of Land Management, "South Pass National Historic Landmark" (Wyoming): a broad, gentle
  crossing of the Continental Divide, over 7,000 feet above sea level.
- <a id="fort-bridger"></a>**fort-bridger**: Wyoming State Parks and Historic Sites, "Fort Bridger State Historic
  Site": the trading post started by Jim Bridger and Louis Vasquez in 1843. The National Park Service Oregon National
  Historic Trail pages on Fort Bridger agree.
- <a id="fort-hall"></a>**fort-hall**: National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail (nps.gov/oreg), pages on
  Fort Hall and on Soda Springs (carbonated springs that travelers tasted); Idaho State Historical Society
  (history.idaho.gov), the pages on Fort Hall and on the Oregon Trail in Idaho.
- <a id="snake-river"></a>**snake-river**: Idaho Department of Parks and Recreation, "Three Island Crossing State
  Park": wagon parties could ford the Snake River from island to island; parties that did not cross took the
  longer, drier route along the south side. Also the Oregon-California Trails Association.
- <a id="blue-mountains"></a>**blue-mountains**: Bureau of Land Management, "National Historic Oregon Trail
  Interpretive Center" (Flagstaff Hill, Baker City, Oregon); U.S. Forest Service, Umatilla National Forest, the page on
  the Oregon Trail's Blue Mountain Crossing: pine forests after the dry sagebrush, and wagon ruts still visible.
- <a id="the-dalles"></a>**the-dalles**: National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail (nps.gov/oreg), the
  pages on The Dalles and the Barlow Road: parties rafted down the Columbia or took the toll road, which opened in
  1846. Also Oregon Historical Society, the Oregon Encyclopedia (oregonencyclopedia.org), entries on The Dalles and the
  Barlow Road.
- <a id="willamette-valley"></a>**willamette-valley**: National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail
  (nps.gov/oreg), the pages on Oregon City and the end of the trail; End of the Oregon Trail Interpretive Center
  (Oregon City); Oregon Historical Society, the Oregon Encyclopedia: Oregon City stands beside Willamette Falls, and
  the valley's rich soil drew farmers.

## Nations

Nations are named the way they name themselves. Their own websites are the first source, because they say best who
they are and where they live today. Tribal pages move, so search the nation's name plus "official website" if a link
has changed. A parent's eye on the wording of these eight facts is welcome before release (SPEC section 1).

- <a id="kansa"></a>**kansa**: Kaw Nation (the Kaw Nation of Oklahoma's own website), the history page: the Kansas
  River and the state of Kansas are named for the Kansa people. Also Kansas Historical Society, Kansapedia, the entry
  on the Kansa (Kaw) Indians.
- <a id="pawnee"></a>**pawnee**: Pawnee Nation of Oklahoma (pawneenation.org), the history and culture pages: farming
  villages and bison hunting; the nation's headquarters are in Pawnee, Oklahoma. Also Nebraska State Historical
  Society, the pages on the Pawnee.
- <a id="lakota"></a>**lakota**: Oglala Sioux Tribe and Standing Rock Sioux Tribe (the tribes' own websites; Standing
  Rock is at standingrock.org); National Park Service, Fort Laramie National Historic Site (nps.gov/fola), the pages
  on the Plains peoples and the fur trade: bison robes and the Lakota's place on the northern Plains.
- <a id="shoshone"></a>**shoshone**: Eastern Shoshone Tribe and Northern Arapaho Tribe, the Wind River Reservation in
  Wyoming (the tribes' own sites); National
  Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail pages on South Pass and Fort Bridger.
- <a id="bannock"></a>**bannock**: Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, Fort Hall Reservation, Idaho (the tribes' own website),
  the history pages on Bannock bison-hunting journeys and the Fort Hall Reservation; National Park Service,
  Yellowstone National Park, the page on the Bannock Trail.
- <a id="nez-perce"></a>**nez-perce**: Nez Perce Tribe (nezperce.org), the pages on history and culture: the people
  call themselves Nimiipuu, the tribe is based in Lapwai, Idaho, and is known for its horses and the Appaloosa; National
  Park Service, Nez Perce National Historical Park (nps.gov/nepe).
- <a id="cayuse"></a>**cayuse**: Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation (ctuir.org), the history and
  culture pages: the Cayuse, Umatilla and Walla Walla peoples; the Cayuse and their horses. Also Oregon Historical
  Society, the Oregon Encyclopedia, the entry on the Cayuse, and Merriam-Webster, "cayuse" (a word for a horse).
- <a id="chinook"></a>**chinook**: Chinook Indian Nation (chinooknation.org), the history and culture pages: the lower
  Columbia River homeland, the nation's long record as traders, and where the nation is based today; Oregon Historical
  Society, the Oregon Encyclopedia, the entries on the Chinook and on Chinookan peoples of the Columbia River.

## Things we deliberately did not say

- **No miles between stops, no counts of travelers, no dates of treaties.** Sources disagree or give ranges.
- **No claims about who stood exactly where on the trail.** The game says which nations have homelands in the
  region the trail crosses, and the trading posts are a game, not a record of one event.
- **The Dalles was a gathering place of several Chinookan peoples (the Wasco and Wishram among them).** The game
  names the Chinook, and says "Chinookan peoples" in the fact so that
  the wider family is included.
- **Soda Springs** is folded into the Fort Hall leg (the game has twelve legs); its fact is read at Fort Hall.

## Trail cards (v1.1)

The 40 trail cards in the Journal's Cards page (src/content/cards.js). The twelve landmark cards and the eight
nation cards each repeat one sentence of the lookout or trading-post fact of the same name, so they are sourced by the
entries above (the card's `source` points at the same anchor). The eight companion cards are story characters; their
lines come from the companions' own trail chatter and need no source. The twelve cards below are new: an animal or
plant of the trail with one plain, checkable line. Numbers are the agencies' usual round figures, and the game says
"about" where they are estimates.

- <a id="card-bison"></a>**bison**: National Park Service, Yellowstone National Park, the bison pages: the bison is
  the largest land mammal in North America, and bison can run about 35 miles an hour.
- <a id="card-pronghorn"></a>**pronghorn**: National Park Service, the pronghorn pages (for example Wind Cave and
  Yellowstone): the pronghorn is the fastest land animal in North America, up to about 55 miles an hour in a sprint.
- <a id="card-coyote"></a>**coyote**: National Park Service, the coyote pages (for example Yellowstone and Rocky
  Mountain National Parks): coyotes communicate with howls and yips, and live across many habitats, from deserts and
  prairies to mountains.
- <a id="card-bald-eagle"></a>**bald-eagle**: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the bald eagle fact sheet: a wingspan of
  about six to seven and a half feet, and fish as the main food, caught with the talons.
- <a id="card-prairie-dog"></a>**prairie-dog**: National Park Service, Wind Cave and Badlands National Parks, the
  prairie dog pages: they live in underground colonies called towns, give alarm barks, and belong to the squirrel family.
- <a id="card-rattlesnake"></a>**rattlesnake**: National Park Service, the pages on wildlife safety: keep a respectful
  distance from wild animals. The game says only "give it room" and nothing more about the animal.
- <a id="card-sagebrush"></a>**sagebrush**: Bureau of Land Management and the National Park Service, the sagebrush
  steppe pages: a gray-green shrub of the dry West with strongly aromatic leaves.
- <a id="card-wild-plum"></a>**wild-plum**: Kansas Historical Society, Kansapedia, the entries on wild plums and
  prairie plants, and the Missouri Department of Conservation's field guide entry on the wild plum (Prunus americana):
  it grows in thickets and its small fruit ripens in late summer.
- <a id="card-camas"></a>**camas**: National Park Service, Nez Perce National Historical Park, the pages on camas:
  blue flowers, and a bulb that Native peoples of the Northwest, the Nez Perce among them, have long cooked in earth
  ovens and still gather. Also the Nez Perce Tribe's own website.
- <a id="card-salmon"></a>**salmon**: National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, NOAA Fisheries, the Pacific
  salmon pages: salmon hatch in fresh water, live in the ocean, and return to the river where they were born.
- <a id="card-mule-deer"></a>**mule-deer**: National Park Service, the mule deer pages (for example Rocky Mountain
  and Zion National Parks): named for their large ears, which resemble a mule's.
- <a id="card-jackrabbit"></a>**jackrabbit**: National Park Service, the black-tailed jackrabbit pages (for example
  Death Valley and Great Basin National Parks): jackrabbits are hares, and their long ears help them hear predators
  and give off heat.

## People of the trail (v1.2)

The 35 people in the stop screens' "Talk" row (src/content/people.js). The people are story characters: the names (Mr. Hale,
Clara, Danny, Hannah and the rest) are invented, and nobody is a real person or a copy of one. What they SAY that is
factual is limited to what a source below backs up, and most of it repeats a lookout or a nation fact already sourced
above. The nations' guides and traders are named for their nation ("a Kansa guide"), never given an invented personal name,
and say only the nation's own facts (the nation entries above) in the present tense where that is true today. Small gifts
(a bag of beans, a trade, a card, a puzzle) are game rules, not history. Where a line is a rule of thumb the game uses
("one blanket for every two people", "keep a spare part") it is advice in the story, not a claim about the trail.

- <a id="people-independence"></a>**independence** (Mr. Hale the outfitter, Clara the wagon-maker's daughter, Amos the
  teamster): National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail (nps.gov/oreg), the pages on outfitting at the Missouri
  River towns, and the Oregon-California Trails Association (octa-trails.org), the "Trail" pages: families bought flour,
  bacon and beans before leaving; wagons waited for the spring grass to grow tall enough to feed the oxen; wheels, axles
  and wagon tongues were the parts that broke. Same sources as the independence lookout above.
- <a id="people-kansas-river"></a>**kansas-river** (Will the ferryman's son, a Kansa guide, Mrs. Ruth with her diary):
  Kansas Historical Society, Kansapedia, the entries on ferries and crossings in Kansas (local families ran ferries that
  carried wagons for a fee) and on the Kansa (Kaw) Indians; Kaw Nation (the nation's own website): the river and the state
  are named for the Kansa, and the Kaw Nation is based in Oklahoma today. Travelers' diaries: National Park Service and
  OCTA, the pages on diaries and journals kept on the trail.
- <a id="people-fort-kearny"></a>**fort-kearny** (Danny the soldier's son, Mr. Hollis the blacksmith, a Pawnee guide):
  Nebraska State Historical Society (history.nebraska.gov), Fort Kearny State Historical Park: founded 1848 to help
  travelers, with a blacksmith shop and a post office; Pawnee Nation of Oklahoma (pawneenation.org): corn, beans and
  squash in river villages, bison hunting, headquarters in Pawnee, Oklahoma. The same as the fort-kearny lookout and the
  Pawnee nation entry.
- <a id="people-chimney-rock"></a>**chimney-rock** (Hannah with her diary, a Lakota guide, Mr. Pike the trader): National
  Park Service, Chimney Rock National Historic Site (nps.gov/chro): the spire's height and the travelers' diaries; Oglala
  Sioux Tribe and Standing Rock Sioux Tribe (the tribes' own websites): the nation line is the lakota entry above. Bison
  robes as a trade good and as warm bedding: National Park Service, Fort Laramie National Historic Site (nps.gov/fola),
  the pages on the robe trade.
- <a id="people-fort-laramie"></a>**fort-laramie** (Tom the soldier's son, a Lakota trader, Old Jed the mountain man):
  National Park Service, Fort Laramie National Historic Site (nps.gov/fola): a trading post from 1834 for furs and bison
  robes, bought by the army in 1849, and the pages on the fur trade and the mountain men (beaver pelts were made into felt
  hats, which drove the trade). Soldiers' families lived at army posts of the period; "my father is a soldier here" is a
  story detail, not a claim about one family.
- <a id="people-independence-rock"></a>**independence-rock** (Lucy with her paint pot, a Shoshone guide): National Park
  Service, Oregon National Historic Trail (nps.gov/oreg), the page on Independence Rock; Wyoming State Parks and Historic
  Sites, "Independence Rock State Historic Site": travelers carved or painted their names, and some can still be seen;
  Eastern Shoshone Tribe and Northern Arapaho Tribe (the Wind River Reservation, Wyoming): the shoshone entry above.
- <a id="people-south-pass"></a>**south-pass** (Nell at the top of the pass, Mr. Silas the freight driver): National Park
  Service, Oregon National Historic Trail, the page on South Pass; Bureau of Land Management, "South Pass National
  Historic Landmark": a wide, gentle crossing of the Continental Divide, over 7,000 feet above sea level; U.S. Geological
  Survey, the Continental Divide: streams east of the Divide drain toward the Atlantic side and streams west of it toward
  the Pacific side. Cold nights at that height are the climate of the high Rockies (the game's blanket rule is a rule of
  thumb, not a historical figure).
- <a id="people-fort-bridger"></a>**fort-bridger** (Mr. Finch the clerk, Greta the blacksmith's helper, a Shoshone
  trader): Wyoming State Parks and Historic Sites, "Fort Bridger State Historic Site": started in 1843 by Jim Bridger and
  Louis Vasquez as a trading post where wagon parties traded, bought supplies and mended wagons. The Shoshone traded with
  travelers there: the shoshone nation entry and the National Park Service Oregon National Historic Trail pages on Fort
  Bridger.
- <a id="people-fort-hall"></a>**fort-hall** (Wesley at the springs, a Bannock guide): National Park Service, Oregon
  National Historic Trail, pages on Fort Hall and on Soda Springs (carbonated springs that travelers tasted); Idaho State
  Historical Society (history.idaho.gov); Shoshone-Bannock Tribes (the tribes' own website): the bannock entry above.
- <a id="people-snake-river"></a>**snake-river** (Josie at the crossing, Mr. Dell): Idaho Department of Parks and
  Recreation, "Three Island Crossing State Park": wagon parties could wade across the Snake River from island to island,
  and parties that did not cross took a longer, drier trail on the south side. Keeping flour dry and a coil of rope handy
  is the game's river advice, in the story.
- <a id="people-blue-mountains"></a>**blue-mountains** (a Nez Perce horse trader, a Cayuse guide, Mr. Pruitt): Nez Perce
  Tribe (nezperce.org) and the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation (ctuir.org): the nez-perce and cayuse
  entries above, word for word in meaning (Nimiipuu, the Appaloosa, Lapwai; the word cayuse, the Umatilla Reservation);
  Bureau of Land Management, National Historic Oregon Trail Interpretive Center, and U.S. Forest Service, Umatilla
  National Forest: pine forests and shade after the dry sagebrush.
- <a id="people-the-dalles"></a>**the-dalles** (a Chinook trader, Ben the toll keeper's son, Mr. Teague the raft builder):
  Chinook Indian Nation (chinooknation.org): Chinookan peoples on the Columbia River, traders who traveled by canoe, the
  nation based in Washington state today; National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail, the pages on The Dalles
  and the Barlow Road; Oregon Historical Society, the Oregon Encyclopedia: parties rafted down the Columbia or took the
  Barlow Road, a toll road that opened in 1846.
- <a id="people-willamette-valley"></a>**willamette-valley** (Mrs. Ingrid the farmer, a Chinook guide, Beth the
  shopkeeper's daughter): National Park Service, Oregon National Historic Trail, the pages on Oregon City and the end of
  the trail; End of the Oregon Trail Interpretive Center (Oregon City); Oregon Historical Society, the Oregon
  Encyclopedia: Oregon City stands beside Willamette Falls, and the valley's rich soil drew farmers; Chinook Indian
  Nation (above) for the guide's first line.

## Supplies, skills and rivers (v1.2): rules, not history

The wagon's food by kind (flour, bacon, beans), the five-arrow hunt, a blanket for every two people on cold legs, tired
animals, the second yoke, the breakdown that needs a spare wheel, axle or tongue, and the ford, the float and the ferry are
game rules built from the general picture the National Park Service and the Oregon-California Trails Association give:
parties carried flour, bacon and beans; oxen and mules pulled the wagons and tired; wheels, axles and tongues broke; rivers
were forded, floated on rafts or crossed by ferry for a fee. The numbers (percentages, pounds, miles) are the game's, tuned
by tools/economy-sim.mjs, and are not read aloud as facts about the trail.

## Fact check (October 31, 2026)

Every nation fact and every lookout fact was checked again against the pages below. How it was done, honestly: the
checking was done from search results that quote or summarise these pages; the build machine could not open nps.gov,
history.nebraska.gov or the nations' own sites directly (its network blocks them), so a few of the nations' own pages are
named but not read line by line. Where the only support was a secondary page, the verdict says so. A parent who wants to
check a fact should start with the first address in its row.

Verdicts: confirmed (a reputable page says it), corrected (the game's text was changed), could not verify (kept as it was,
or softened, and flagged).
