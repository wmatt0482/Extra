Learning Lab

Why it works

Every part of Spell Jump and Trail Blazer exists because a learning technique has evidence for it. For each one: what the research found, and where it lives in the game. Where the evidence is thin or comes from a different age group, that is noted.

  1. Retrieval practice

    Spell from memory, from audio, never from a visible word.

    The evidence
    Rated a highest-utility learning technique in a major review (Dunlosky et al. 2013). A 2018 classroom study found retrieval practice beat rainbow writing for spelling, including 5 weeks later.
    In the game
    Every jump starts with a word spoken aloud and spelled from memory. No word is ever on screen while your child spells it.

  2. Spaced practice

    Practice spread over days, not crammed into one.

    The evidence
    Also rated highest-utility by Dunlosky et al. 2013; spaced retrieval helps children's word learning.
    In the game
    Words move through boxes with growing gaps: same session, next day, 2 days, 4 days. A word counts as Mastered only after 3 first-try successes on at least 2 different days. Past lists come back 2 and 4 weeks later.

  3. Test, study, test

    Pretest, study only what you missed, then retest.

    The evidence
    About twice as many studies favor test-study-test over study-then-test, from Horn's era onward; it is the weekly cycle Gentry recommends.
    In the game
    A new list starts with a quick Qualifier (the pretest). Known words skip ahead; missed words become Focus Words and get most of the practice. A Friday Test closes the week, and the parent report shows pretest vs test.

  4. Immediate self-correction

    Correct right after each word, with the child doing the fixing.

    The evidence
    Horn's corrected-test research; later single-case studies found correcting after each word beat correcting after the whole list.
    In the game
    A miss is checked at once. Your child sees which letter positions differ, then fixes it themselves.

  5. Cover-Copy-Compare

    A short routine for fixing a missed word.

    The evidence
    An established, evidence-based self-management procedure (Joseph et al. 2012 review).
    In the game
    After a miss: Compare (differences marked), Copy (type it from the model), Cover (model hidden, type it once more from memory).

  6. No exposure to misspellings

    Wrong spellings are kept out of sight.

    The evidence
    Reading misspellings lowers later spelling accuracy for both good and poor spellers, for at least a week (Dixon 1997; Brown 1988).
    In the game
    A wrong attempt is shown for at most 3 seconds with the differences marked, then cleared. The game never offers wrong spellings as choices; "pick the right one" is deliberately absent.

  7. Simultaneous Oral Spelling

    Say the letter names while writing.

    The evidence
    A multisensory routine from Gillingham and Stillman, with reported benefits for young and struggling spellers.
    In the game
    Each typed letter is spoken by name. After a miss, the correct word is spelled aloud letter by letter with the tiles lighting in sync. Letter rings in the air say their letter.

  8. Look-Say-Cover-Write-Check

    For the very first meeting with a new word.

    The evidence
    A randomized trial with Year 3 children found it as effective as a phonological program on the taught words, but weaker at transferring to new words.
    In the game
    First encounter only: Look (the word shown with its pattern chunk highlighted and syllables marked), Say, Cover, Write, Check.

  9. Patterns and word parts

    Teaching the spelling pattern, not just the word.

    The evidence
    Explicit pattern instruction transfers to new words better than rote routines.
    In the game
    An optional "pattern of the week" (for example ough or -tion) and per-word syllables from the parent, highlighted during the Look and Fix steps.

  10. Interleaving

    Mixing kinds of words instead of drilling one kind at a time.

    The evidence
    Third graders made fewer errors after interleaved than after blocked practice (Klimovich & Richter 2025).
    In the game
    The scheduler mixes patterns and mixes in 20% past-list words, and never gives the same word twice in a row.

  11. Instant feedback, few new words

    Know right away, and don't take on too much at once.

    The evidence
    Feedback beats no feedback, and practitioner guidance is to introduce only a few new words per session.
    In the game
    Every word is checked the moment your child taps LAUNCH. At most 5 new words a day, and a daily mission of about 8 words (8 to 10 minutes of play).

  12. Intrinsic integration

    The learning action is the game action.

    The evidence
    Children learned more, and chose to play 7 times longer, with games where the learning was built into the play itself (Habgood & Ainsworth 2011).
    In the game
    Spelling sets the launch power and nitro, letters are the rings, and the Friday score sets the power of the Mega Run. There is no "do homework, then play".

Trail Blazer: Grade 4 math facts

In Trail Blazer, Math Gates stop the race. The goal is facts that are known and fast (the times tables to 12 × 12, the division facts that undo them, + and − within 20), so a child's working memory is free for the bigger problem. The game finds where your child is before the first race, works on the next families that are ready, keeps the gates at the level where they are still learning, and watches the kind of mistakes they make.

  1. Retrieval, not recognition

    Type the answer from memory; never pick it from choices.

    The evidence
    Taking a test improved long-term retention more than studying again (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, with college students). A meta-analysis of math-fact fluency programs for children found practice that asks for the answer, with a model to compare to, among the most effective parts (Codding, Burns & Lukito 2011).
    In the game
    At each gate the fact appears in big type and is read aloud ("seven times eight"); your child types the answer on a number pad. There is no multiple choice anywhere.

  2. Start where the child is

    A short placement check after the first race, instead of starting everyone at 2 × 1.

    The evidence
    Skills move through phases, from getting it right at all (acquisition) to fast (fluency) to keeping it (maintenance), and practice that matches the phase works best (Haring & Eaton 1978; Codding, Burns & Lukito 2011). Finding a child's instructional level in math facts first, then practicing there, is standard in school intervention work (Burns, VanDerHeyden & Jiban 2006). Adaptive tests that step up after right answers and down after wrong ones reach the same precision with far fewer questions (Weiss & Kingsbury 1984).
    In the game
    The Pit Crew Check runs before the first race (Start, or "Race first" and it asks again next time). It starts mid-ladder at ×6 ×9, climbs a tier when two facts are right and quick and drops on a miss, then zooms: two more facts per family in the tier it landed on and the one above, so it can tell ×6 from ×9, plus one division twin per fluent family. Usually 12 to 18 facts, never more than 24, no clock and no score on screen. It ends on the Start line: Fluent, Working on, Up next. It is a placement screen, not a validated test; it re-runs on its own after two weeks or when the placement looks stale, and the parent can run it any time.

  3. A ladder, easy and rule-based facts first

    ×2 ×5 ×10, then ×3 ×4, then ×6 ×9, then ×7 ×8, then ×11 ×12, then division.

    The evidence
    Children learn the combinations with a pattern or rule first and lean on them for the rest (Baroody 2006). Fourth graders taught fact strategies alongside timed practice did better than with timed practice alone, including on the hard facts (Woodward 2006). A division fact is easiest once its multiplication twin is known.
    In the game
    Auto mode works on the lowest unfinished tier first, so a shaky ×6 is never skipped for ×8. Within a tier the weakest family goes first. A division family joins the gates two facts after its multiplication twin is known.

  4. Spacing and incremental rehearsal

    Facts return over days, and a new fact is practiced in between known ones.

    The evidence
    Practice spread over time is remembered longer than massed practice (Cepeda et al. 2006, a review of hundreds of studies, mostly with adults). Rehearsing a new multiplication fact among known ones, with growing gaps, raised fluency in children who struggled with math (Burns 2005, a small study).
    In the game
    Facts move through boxes (same day, next day, 2 days, 4 days), one step a day at most. A new fact comes back after 1, then 2, then 3 known facts. Mastered means right on at least 2 different days.

  5. Fluency: right, then fast

    Answers in about 3 seconds, without a clock on screen.

    The evidence
    Drill builds speed only once a fact can be recalled at all, and a fast answer is the sign it is recalled rather than counted out (Hasselbring, Goin & Bransford 1988). Fourth graders taught fact strategies plus timed practice did better than with timed practice alone (Woodward 2006).
    In the game
    A right answer within 3 seconds is a ⚡ Quick boost. A fact is Fluent after quick answers on 2 different days. Slow and right still opens the gate with a boost, and there is never a penalty for being slow.

  6. Cover-Copy-Compare with a strategy

    See it, hear it, hide it, write it from memory.

    The evidence
    Cover-Copy-Compare worked across skills, math facts included, students and settings (Skinner, McLaughlin & Logan 1997). Feedback helps most when it says how to get it right, not just whether it was right (Hattie & Timperley 2007).
    In the game
    A wrong answer is cleared within half a second. The fact with its answer and a hint (for 9 × 7: "10 × 7 is 70, take away 7"; for 56 ÷ 7: "7 × ? = 56") is shown and read aloud, then covered, and your child types it from memory to open the gate. The fact comes back later in the same session.

  7. Learning from the kind of mistake

    A miss is sorted by what went wrong, and the hint and the practice follow.

    The evidence
    Most multiplication errors are not random: the commonest is a neighbouring fact from the same table (7 × 8 answered 48 or 63), and errors cluster on the large operands (Campbell & Graham 1985). Systematic error patterns tell a teacher what to teach next (Ashlock 2010).
    In the game
    Every miss is classified: neighbouring fact, one group off, digits swapped, the other operation, division missed while its multiplication is known, big numbers, or slow but right. The pattern picks the hint ("7 × 7 is 49, then one more 7 is 56"), nudges which families stay in rotation, and shows up in the parent Report in plain words ("Misses cluster in ×7 and ×8").

  8. Interleaving

    Tables mixed, not drilled one at a time.

    The evidence
    Shuffling different kinds of math problems together improved later test scores over practicing one kind at a time (Rohrer & Taylor 2007, with college students).
    In the game
    About 6 gates in 10 are the two families being worked on, 3 are known families coming back for spacing, and 1 is a quick check on a mastered one. Multiplication, division and the parent's own problems mix within a race, and the same fact never comes twice in a row.

  9. Move on by evidence, and come back if it slips

    A family is finished when the child has shown it is known, not after a set number of days.

    The evidence
    Teaching to a mastery criterion before moving on raised achievement across a large body of classroom studies (Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990, a meta-analysis), with the biggest gains for weaker students. Brief, frequent measurement of what a child can actually do, used to adjust teaching right away, is the core of curriculum-based measurement (Deno 1985; Fuchs & Fuchs 1986). Spaced reviews are what keep it (Cepeda et al. 2006).
    In the game
    A family leaves the rotation the same sitting once every fact has been answered right, three quarters of the answers were quick, and none of the last eight was a miss. Every race also has one scout gate: a never-asked fact from the next tier up. Three quick scouts with no miss vouch a family the child never had to grind. A slip brings a family back, and every 15 races or 7 days six mastered facts return as a retention check.

  10. Keep it at the learning level

    Mostly right, never all right, never a wall.

    The evidence
    Children stayed on task and finished more work when the material was at their instructional level, where most items are already known and a few are being learned, than when it was too easy or too hard (Gickling & Armstrong 1978). A review of drill studies found the same: practice works best with a high share of known items among the new ones (Burns 2004). Fast gameplay that stays easy is not practice; it is screen time.
    In the game
    After every gate the game looks at the last 12 answers. Above 92% right and mostly quick, it moves up: families the child has beaten leave at once and two scouts come next race. Between 70% and 92%, it holds. Below 70%, it narrows to one family, stops scouting, and steps a family the child is drowning in back to the one its strategy leans on, so ×8 goes back to ×4 for a while. The child sees only "Right now: on target"; parents see the dial and its history.

  11. Immediate, specific feedback

    Know within the same second, and always see the correct fact.

    The evidence
    Feedback that tells the learner how to get it right beats a bare right or wrong (Hattie & Timperley 2007). Seeing the correct answer right after a miss is part of what made fact-fluency programs work (Codding, Burns & Lukito 2011).
    In the game
    Right or wrong shows the moment your child taps GO, always with the correct fact. A wrong answer is never left on screen, and no count of wrong answers is ever shown to the child.

  12. Hearing the fact

    Every fact and every hint is read aloud as it appears.

    The evidence
    This one is a design choice more than a finding. Reading the fact aloud means a child who reads slowly is not held back at the gate, and hearing "seven times eight is fifty-six" alongside the symbols gives the memory two routes in. The evidence that hearing facts helps on its own is thin.
    In the game
    The fact is spoken when it appears, the fix-it fact and hint are spoken too, and a Hear it button repeats it. Parents can turn it off in Options.

  13. Progress you can see, learning that is the game

    The Fact Grid, a small daily goal, and facts that win races.

    The evidence
    Tracking progress regularly, especially with a graph, raised achievement (Fuchs & Fuchs 1986, a meta-analysis). Children learned more and played longer when the learning was the game action (Habgood & Ainsworth 2011).
    In the game
    The Fact Grid colors every fact New, Learning, Almost, Mastered or Fluent; the Start line shows Fluent, Working on and Up next; parents get a Report with patterns and slowest facts. A daily mission of 12 gate answers is about 10 minutes of play. Facts open the gates and fill the boost tank, and the race clock stops during a question, so fast answers, not fast reading, win the race.

Fraction Fright: fractions as numbers

In Fraction Fright the road is a number line. The goal is that a child sees a fraction as a number with a place between 0 and 1 (Grade 4, CCSS 4.NF; denominators 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 100), not as slices of a pie. Each zombie rises out of the fog at its fraction's spot, hidden until it has walked most of the way: the only way to find it early is the number, and a zombie that steps out of the fog is the true spot showing itself, a second form of the feedback below. Throws at a zombie already in view are kept apart and never count toward a secure street. Version 1.3 has the Zombie Lab and the first two streets, with twins (two spellings of one number at one spot), a boss that shows its number three ways, and a short arcade bonus round (the Critter Stampede) that a wave with 80 % hits earns; comparing, sums, decimals and fractions past 1 come next. A hit blasts the zombie into cartoon green goo (never blood, never the words kill or die); Gentle mode in the Parent Zone turns that into a cure and a neighbour walking home. The research below was consulted through abstracts and secondary summaries, and the game is our reading of it, not a tested intervention.

  1. A fraction is a number on a line

    The road is a number line; pies are never the main picture.

    The evidence
    How well children place fractions on a number line predicts their later math, including algebra, across studies and countries (Siegler, Thompson & Schneider 2011; Siegler et al. 2012; the Schneider et al. 2018 meta-analysis). Number-line activities improved fraction magnitude understanding more than area models such as pies (Hamdan & Gunderson 2017), and the US practice guide recommends number lines as a central picture (Siegler et al. 2010).
    In the game
    Zombies shuffle down a road that is a number line from 0 to 1. The scout calls a fraction; your child drags along the road and lobs slime at that spot. Nothing else decides a hit: only where the slime lands (Habgood & Ainsworth 2011).

  2. Show the true spot after every throw

    The flag, the fraction drawn as jumps from 0, and how close it was.

    The evidence
    In an estimation study, practice with feedback after each answer improved children's number-line estimates, and the same practice without it did not (Fazio, Kennedy & Siegler 2016).
    In the game
    After every throw the true spot gets a flag and the fraction is drawn as equal jumps from 0 (three jumps of a quarter for ¾), with "92 % close". The words are always positive: never "wrong". A second try at the same zombie gets an arrow toward the spot, then the denominator's ticks for one throw.

  3. Benchmarks stay labelled

    0, ½ and 1 are always named on the road; finer ticks come and go.

    The evidence
    Labelled benchmarks on the number line helped third and fourth graders estimate; unlabelled ticks did not (Peeters et al. 2017).
    In the game
    0, ½ and 1 are always labelled. The ticks for quarters or eighths appear only as a hint after a miss, for one throw.

  4. A short lesson first

    The Zombie Lab: about a minute, before the first street.

    The evidence
    A number-line game helped fraction understanding transfer when it came with a short instruction phase, and the same game without it did not (Nuraydin, Stricker & Schneider 2022).
    In the game
    Before the first street, the Zombie Lab: the road is a number line; drag to cut it into 4 equal jumps and name them; lob slime at ¾; the same with thirds; then ½ sits in the same spot on every road (2/4, 3/6 and 4/8 line up). At most 8 words on screen at a time, read aloud. Its three throws count as the first throws.

  5. Accuracy, never speed

    No timers, no countdowns; the zombies are slow.

    The evidence
    The fraction interventions that worked focused on understanding fraction size, not speed (Fuchs et al. 2013, 2014, 2016; Siegler et al. 2010).
    In the game
    What is scored and adapted on is how close a throw lands. A zombie takes about 20 seconds to reach the fence (never under 14); if one gets there, it knocks, takes a pumpkin and shuffles off. Nothing else happens.

  6. Start wide, then narrow

    The aiming zone is 10 % of the road, then 5 %, then 3 %.

    The evidence
    A wide target early keeps children playing; difficulty that rises through the math, not through speed, keeps them in the learning zone (design guidance drawn from Lomas et al. 2013 and 2017).
    In the game
    Each street starts with a ±10 % zone. When a street is working (6 or more of the last 12 throws, 60 % on target) the zone narrows to ±5 %, and when secure (8 plain throws, 80 % on target, 97.5 % close on average) to ±3 %. Early on, the slime bounces in unit jumps before it lands (the bounce scaffold).

  7. The child picks the street

    A ★ suggests one; a harder street once opened is never closed.

    The evidence
    Letting players choose their own difficulty kept them motivated in an online fraction game (Lomas et al. 2017).
    In the game
    Maple Street (halves and quarters) opens with the Lab; Oak Lane (thirds, sixths, eighths, then fifths, tenths, twelfths) opens when Maple Street is working. The ★ marks the lowest open street that is not secure yet; the child chooses.

  8. Big effects only on success, effort counts

    The thunk, the slow-mo and the splat belong to the right spot.

    The evidence
    Juicy feedback was most motivating when it followed success (Kao et al. 2024). Rewarding effort and strategy, not only right answers, kept children trying (O'Rourke et al. 2014, "Brain Points").
    In the game
    A bullseye gets the big thunk, a short slow-mo and confetti; a hit a thunk and a splat; a near an edge splat and a nudge arrow; a miss a soft plop. A near followed by a hit on the same zombie earns a bonus ("close, then cured").

  9. No meters, no streak

    The horde is the progress.

    The evidence
    A progress meter cut engagement by about 15 % in a large online experiment (Lomas et al.), and optional collectibles reduced retention (Andersen et al. 2011). The UK Children's Code lists streaks among the nudges to avoid.
    In the game
    During a wave there is no timer, no bar and no counter: the zombies left on the road are the progress. Fraction Fright has no streak. Between waves, the counter grows the title's street for every 10 zombies zapped (neighbours cured, in Gentle mode).
