Learning Lab

Why it works

Every part of Spell Jump, and of Trail Blazer's math facts, exists because a learning technique has evidence for it. For each one: what the research found, and where it lives in the game. Where the evidence is thin or comes from a different age group, that is noted.

  1. Retrieval practice

    Spell from memory, from audio, never from a visible word.

    The evidence
    Rated a highest-utility learning technique in a major review (Dunlosky et al. 2013). A 2018 classroom study found retrieval practice beat rainbow writing for spelling, including 5 weeks later.
    In the game
    Every jump starts with a word spoken aloud and spelled from memory. No word is ever on screen while your child spells it.

  2. Spaced practice

    Practice spread over days, not crammed into one.

    The evidence
    Also rated highest-utility by Dunlosky et al. 2013; spaced retrieval helps children's word learning.
    In the game
    Words move through boxes with growing gaps: same session, next day, 2 days, 4 days. A word counts as Mastered only after 3 first-try successes on at least 2 different days. Past lists come back 2 and 4 weeks later.

  3. Test, study, test

    Pretest, study only what you missed, then retest.

    The evidence
    About twice as many studies favor test-study-test over study-then-test, from Horn's era onward; it is the weekly cycle Gentry recommends.
    In the game
    A new list starts with a quick Qualifier (the pretest). Known words skip ahead; missed words become Focus Words and get most of the practice. A Friday Test closes the week, and the parent report shows pretest vs test.

  4. Immediate self-correction

    Correct right after each word, with the child doing the fixing.

    The evidence
    Horn's corrected-test research; later single-case studies found correcting after each word beat correcting after the whole list.
    In the game
    A miss is checked at once. Your child sees which letter positions differ, then fixes it themselves.

  5. Cover-Copy-Compare

    A short routine for fixing a missed word.

    The evidence
    An established, evidence-based self-management procedure (Joseph et al. 2012 review).
    In the game
    After a miss: Compare (differences marked), Copy (type it from the model), Cover (model hidden, type it once more from memory).

  6. No exposure to misspellings

    Wrong spellings are kept out of sight.

    The evidence
    Reading misspellings lowers later spelling accuracy for both good and poor spellers, for at least a week (Dixon 1997; Brown 1988).
    In the game
    A wrong attempt is shown for at most 3 seconds with the differences marked, then cleared. The game never offers wrong spellings as choices; "pick the right one" is deliberately absent.

  7. Simultaneous Oral Spelling

    Say the letter names while writing.

    The evidence
    A multisensory routine from Gillingham and Stillman, with reported benefits for young and struggling spellers.
    In the game
    Each typed letter is spoken by name. After a miss, the correct word is spelled aloud letter by letter with the tiles lighting in sync. Letter rings in the air say their letter.

  8. Look-Say-Cover-Write-Check

    For the very first meeting with a new word.

    The evidence
    A randomized trial with Year 3 children found it as effective as a phonological program on the taught words, but weaker at transferring to new words.
    In the game
    First encounter only: Look (the word shown with its pattern chunk highlighted and syllables marked), Say, Cover, Write, Check.

  9. Patterns and word parts

    Teaching the spelling pattern, not just the word.

    The evidence
    Explicit pattern instruction transfers to new words better than rote routines.
    In the game
    An optional "pattern of the week" (for example ough or -tion) and per-word syllables from the parent, highlighted during the Look and Fix steps.

  10. Interleaving

    Mixing kinds of words instead of drilling one kind at a time.

    The evidence
    Third graders made fewer errors after interleaved than after blocked practice (Klimovich & Richter 2025).
    In the game
    The scheduler mixes patterns and mixes in 20% past-list words, and never gives the same word twice in a row.

  11. Instant feedback, few new words

    Know right away, and don't take on too much at once.

    The evidence
    Feedback beats no feedback, and practitioner guidance is to introduce only a few new words per session.
    In the game
    Every word is checked the moment your child taps LAUNCH. At most 5 new words a day, and a daily mission of about 8 words (8 to 10 minutes of play).

  12. Intrinsic integration

    The learning action is the game action.

    The evidence
    Children learned more, and chose to play 7 times longer, with games where the learning was built into the play itself (Habgood & Ainsworth 2011).
    In the game
    Spelling sets the launch power and nitro, letters are the rings, and the Friday score sets the power of the Mega Run. There is no "do homework, then play".

Trail Blazer: Grade 4 math facts

In Trail Blazer, Math Gates stop the race. The goal is facts that are known and fast (the times tables to 12 × 12, the division facts that undo them, + and − within 20), so a child's working memory is free for the bigger problem.

  1. Retrieval, not recognition

    Type the answer from memory; never pick it from choices.

    The evidence
    Taking a test improved long-term retention more than studying again (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, with college students). A meta-analysis of math-fact fluency programs for children found practice that asks for the answer, with a model to compare to, among the most effective parts (Codding, Burns & Lukito 2011).
    In the game
    At each gate the fact appears in big type and is read aloud ("seven times eight"); your child types the answer on a number pad.

  2. Spacing and incremental rehearsal

    Facts return over days, and a new fact is practiced in between known ones.

    The evidence
    Practice spread over time is remembered longer than massed practice (Cepeda et al. 2006, a review of hundreds of studies, mostly with adults). Rehearsing a new multiplication fact among known ones, with growing gaps, raised fluency in children who struggled with math (Burns 2005, a small study).
    In the game
    Facts move through boxes (same day, next day, 2 days, 4 days), one step a day at most. A new fact comes back after 1, then 2, then 3 known facts. Mastered means right on at least 2 different days.

  3. Fluency: right, then fast

    Answers in about 3 seconds, without a clock on screen.

    The evidence
    Drill builds speed only once a fact can be recalled at all, and a fast answer is the sign it is recalled rather than counted out (Hasselbring, Goin & Bransford 1988). Fourth graders taught fact strategies plus timed practice did better than with timed practice alone (Woodward 2006).
    In the game
    A right answer within 3 seconds is a ⚡ Quick boost. A fact is Fluent after quick answers on 2 different days. Slow and right still opens the gate with a boost.

  4. Cover-Copy-Compare with a strategy

    See it, hear it, hide it, write it from memory.

    The evidence
    Cover-Copy-Compare worked across skills, math facts included, students and settings (Skinner, McLaughlin & Logan 1997). Feedback helps most when it says how to get it right, not just whether it was right (Hattie & Timperley 2007).
    In the game
    A wrong answer is cleared within half a second. The fact with its answer and a hint (for 9 × 7: "10 × 7 is 70, take away 7"; for 56 ÷ 7: "7 × ? = 56") is shown and read aloud, then covered, and your child types it from memory to open the gate. The fact comes back later in the same session.

  5. Interleaving

    Tables mixed, not drilled one at a time.

    The evidence
    Shuffling different kinds of math problems together improved later test scores over practicing one kind at a time (Rohrer & Taylor 2007, with college students).
    In the game
    Once a table has started, its facts mix with the other chosen tables, division and your own problems.

  6. Progress you can see, learning that is the game

    The Fact Grid, and facts that win races.

    The evidence
    Tracking progress regularly, especially with a graph, raised achievement (Fuchs & Fuchs 1986, a meta-analysis). Children learned more and played longer when the learning was the game action (Habgood & Ainsworth 2011).
    In the game
    The Fact Grid colors every fact New, Learning, Almost, Mastered or Fluent, and parents get a Report. Facts open the gates and fill the boost tank; the race clock stops during a question, so fast answers, not fast reading, win the race.
